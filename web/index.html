Check a skin spot in under a minute.

Skin cancer is the most common cancer in the United States, and early detection saves lives. Upload a close-up photo of a mole or mark and Skinsight estimates whether it looks benign or malignant, and which kind of lesion it resembles.

How accurate is it?

Skinsight was tested on 598 lesions it has never seen. All were added to the ISIC Archive after the model was trained. This is what it got right and wrong.

Works best when

  • The photo is a sharp, evenly lit close-up, like a dermoscope image
  • The lesion fills most of the crop
  • You treat the result as a nudge, not a diagnosis

Don't trust it when

  • The photo is blurry, very dark or very bright
  • The spot is small in the frame
  • The answer changes when you re-crop. Small framing changes flipped about 1 in 5 decisions in testing.
  • The image isn't a skin-surface photo. On confocal microscopy it called every image malignant.
  • You have darker skin. Almost all test images with a skin-type label were Fitzpatrick I–II.

About

What's inside

A ResNet-18 with two heads: one predicts benign vs. malignant, and one picks among 11 lesion types from the ISIC diagnosis hierarchy. It was trained in PyTorch on ISIC Archive images. Here it runs as ONNX in your browser via onnxruntime-web, so nothing is uploaded.

Not a doctor

Skinsight is for education. It catches only about half of cancers at its default setting, so a "benign" result does not mean a spot is safe. See a dermatologist about any spot that is new, changing, itching, or bleeding.
